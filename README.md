# Writing Like a Human: A Reference Guide

A working reference for drafting and editing English that reads like a real person wrote it. Keep it open while you write, and run the checklist in section 13 before you publish anything.

## 1. What detectors actually look for

Most detectors don't "recognize" AI. They measure how predictable a piece of text is.

- **Perplexity.** How surprising each next word is. AI keeps picking the most likely word, so its text is very predictable. People make odder choices.
- **Burstiness.** How much sentences vary. AI sentences tend to be similar in length and shape. Human writing jumps around: long, short, a fragment, a rambling one.
- **Style fingerprints.** Classifiers trained on huge amounts of AI output learn its favorite words, transitions, and structures.

So the goal isn't to sound fancy, and it isn't to sound dumb. It's to be **less predictable and more specific**.

One thing to keep in mind: plain vocabulary is good, but *plain and generic* is not. "This product is very good and easy to use" is simple and also completely predictable, and detectors flag that too. Simple words in specific, slightly unexpected combinations is what you're aiming for.

A quick vocabulary rule: if you wouldn't say the word out loud to a friend or coworker, swap it for one you would.

## 2. The golden rule: say something only you would say

This matters more than every other technique in this guide combined. AI writes from the average of everything it has read. You write from your own life and work.

Add these wherever you can:

- **Real numbers and details.** "About 40 minutes," not "a significant amount of time."
- **Named things.** The actual app, the city, the month, the person's job title.
- **What happened to you.** What you tried, what you noticed, what surprised you.
- **Opinions that pick a side.** "I think X is overrated" beats "X has both pros and cons."
- **Things that went wrong.** Complaints, mistakes, "I got this wrong at first."
- **Small asides** that don't strictly need to be there.
- **Uneven attention.** Spend three paragraphs on the thing you care about and one line on the thing you don't. AI gives every point equal weight.

**Test:** if you could paste a paragraph into someone else's article on the same topic and it would still fit, it's too generic. Rewrite it with something only you know.

## 3. Words and phrases to cut (with swaps)

These show up in AI text far more often than in human writing. One or two won't hurt, but a cluster is a giveaway. Use Ctrl+F to hunt for them during editing.

### Words

| AI favorite | Say instead |
|---|---|
| delve (into) | look at, get into, dig into |
| crucial, pivotal, vital, paramount | important, key, big, matters a lot |
| leverage, harness, utilize | use |
| robust | strong, solid, reliable |
| seamless(ly) | smooth, easy, no hassle |
| landscape, realm, sphere | world, field, market (or just drop it) |
| tapestry, symphony, testament to | usually delete the whole metaphor |
| foster, cultivate | build, grow, encourage |
| navigate (a problem) | deal with, handle, get through |
| embark on | start |
| elevate, enhance | improve, make better, boost |
| unlock, unleash | get, open up (or rewrite the sentence) |
| showcase, highlight, underscore | show, point out |
| intricate, multifaceted, nuanced | complicated, tricky, has a few sides |
| comprehensive | full, complete, thorough |
| meticulous(ly) | careful(ly) |
| vibrant, bustling | lively, busy |
| myriad, plethora | lots of, a bunch of, many |
| streamline | simplify, speed up |
| commendable, noteworthy | good, worth mentioning |
| game-changer | big deal, huge help |
| invaluable | really useful |
| resonate (with) | click with, hit home |
| boast (a feature) | has |
| align with | match, fit |
| ensure | make sure |
| facilitate | help, make easier |
| optimal | best |

### Transitions

AI glues every paragraph to the last one with a formal connector. People usually just start the next thought.

| AI | Human |
|---|---|
| Furthermore / Moreover / Additionally | Also, Plus, And (or nothing at all) |
| However | But, Still, or "though" at the end of the sentence |
| Consequently / Therefore / Thus | So |
| Nevertheless | Even so, Still |
| Ultimately | In the end (or nothing) |
| Notably / Importantly | Cut it. If it matters, the reader will notice. |
| In conclusion / To sum up / Overall | Cut the whole summary paragraph |

### Stock phrases (delete on sight)

- In today's fast-paced world / digital age / ever-evolving landscape
- It's important to note that / It's worth mentioning that
- Whether you're a [X] or a [Y], ...
- Let's dive in / Let's explore / Buckle up
- When it comes to ...
- At the end of the day
- A wide range of / a variety of
- Plays a crucial role in
- Stands out as
- Look no further
- Unlock your full potential / Take your [X] to the next level
- I hope this email finds you well
- Please don't hesitate to reach out
- Here's the thing / Here's the kicker (AI picked these up too, so they're overused now)

Also watch for clichés AI leans on: "a double-edged sword," "a breath of fresh air," "stand the test of time," "the icing on the cake."

## 4. Sentence patterns that give AI away

Words are easy to fix. Structures are what sneak through, so pay the most attention here.

- **"It's not just X, it's Y."** Also "This isn't about X. It's about Y." Probably the single most recognizable AI move. Just say Y.
- **Everything in threes.** "Fast, reliable, and affordable." "Clarity, focus, and purpose." Real people list two things, or four, or one. Break up the triplets.
- **Setup, colon, reveal.** "The result? A 30% increase." "The secret: consistency." Fine once. AI does it every few paragraphs.
- **Rhetorical question hooks.** "Ever wondered why...?" "What if I told you...?"
- **"Imagine..."** openers.
- **Balanced hedging.** "While X has its advantages, it also has drawbacks." Pick a side.
- **The -ing tail.** "..., making it an ideal choice for busy families." "..., ensuring a smooth experience." AI loves tacking an -ing clause onto the end to explain why something matters. Cut it or make it its own sentence.
- **"From X to Y" ranges.** "From beginners to experts..." "From morning coffee to late-night snacks..."
- **Template paragraphs.** Topic sentence, two supporting sentences, a wrap-up line. Every time. Too tidy.
- **Restating the question** before answering it.
- **Same opener three times in a row.** "This means... This allows... This ensures..."
- **Abstract nouns with empty intensifiers.** "A truly transformative experience." "A deep sense of fulfillment."

## 5. Rhythm: mix it up

This is burstiness in practice. Look at the sentence lengths in any paragraph you've written. If they're all between 15 and 25 words, rework it.

- Drop a very short sentence after a long one. Two words is fine. "It worked."
- Let one sentence run long, with an aside or two, the way people talk when they're explaining something they actually care about.
- Use a fragment now and then. "Not ideal."
- Vary paragraph length too. A one-line paragraph after a chunky one is completely normal.
- Vary how sentences start: subject first, then a time phrase ("Last week,"), then a conjunction ("But"), then a question.

**Before** (every sentence about the same length):

> Remote work offers employees greater flexibility in managing their daily schedules. It also eliminates the time and cost associated with commuting. However, it can lead to feelings of isolation for some workers. Companies should therefore consider hybrid models that balance these competing factors.

**After:**

> Remote work gave me back about ten hours a week. That's the commute alone. But by month three I was talking to my cat more than to actual adults, which is funny until it isn't. Hybrid is probably the answer for most teams. Two days in the office, maybe three.

## 6. Conversational English toolkit

- **Contractions.** it's, don't, can't, won't, I'd, you'll, they've, that's, wasn't. Text with no contractions reads stiff and generated.
- **Phrasal verbs instead of formal verbs.**

  | Formal | Everyday |
  |---|---|
  | determine | figure out |
  | discover | find out |
  | establish | set up |
  | review | go over |
  | postpone | put off |
  | develop (an idea) | come up with |
  | investigate | look into |
  | encounter | run into |
  | eliminate | get rid of |
  | result in | end up with |

- **Talk to the reader.** Use "you" and "your." If you ask a question, make it one you actually want answered, not a rhetorical hook.
- **First person.** "I" and "we." Say what you did, saw, and think.
- **Discourse markers** (a few, not every sentence): so, anyway, honestly, actually, well, okay, the thing is, to be fair, I mean, turns out.
- **Everyday intensifiers.** pretty, really, super, way ("way better"), kind of, a bit, totally.
- **Natural hedges.** I think, probably, I'm not sure, as far as I know, I could be wrong but.
- **Plain idioms.** worth it, no big deal, hit or miss, a pain, the catch is, not bad at all, took forever, out of the box, in the long run, on the fence.
- **Concrete verbs.** "broke," "grabbed," "tossed," "skimmed" instead of "experienced issues," "acquired," "discarded," "reviewed."

## 7. Informal and non-standard grammar (used on purpose)

Real people bend grammar rules all the time, especially online. A few of these make text feel written by a person instead of generated. **Pick two or three per piece.** Using all of them reads like someone pretending to be casual, which is its own kind of tell.

| Technique | Example | Where it works |
|---|---|---|
| Start with And / But / So / Or | "But nobody told us that." | Almost anywhere, even business writing |
| Sentence fragments | "Worth every yen." "Not great." | Blogs, reviews, emails, social |
| End with a preposition | "That's the part I'm stuck on." | Everywhere |
| Split infinitives | "to really understand" | Everywhere |
| "Who" instead of "whom" | "Who did you send it to?" | Everywhere except very formal writing |
| Singular "they" | "If a customer calls, tell them..." | Everywhere |
| "There's" with a plural | "There's a few things to check." | Casual, speech-like writing |
| Occasional comma splice | "It was late, I was tired." | Casual writing, stories |
| Chained "and" | "We got there at 9 and the line was already out the door and nobody knew why." | Stories and anecdotes |
| Parenthetical asides | "(which, looking back, was a bad idea)" | Blogs, emails |
| Trailing "though" | "It's expensive, though." | Anywhere casual |
| Dropped subject | "Tried it last week. Didn't work." | Social, texts, quick notes |
| kinda / gonna / like | "It's kinda slow." | Social, chat, dialogue only |
| Lowercase and abbreviations | "tbh it's fine" | Social posts and DMs only |
| "Okay so" / "Anyway" openers | "Anyway, back to the setup." | Blogs, social |

**What not to do:** fake typos, random misspellings, or broken grammar that makes the meaning unclear. Readers notice, and it doesn't help much with detectors anyway.

## 8. Punctuation and formatting

- **Em dashes (—).** AI uses them constantly. Keep it to one per piece, or none. Use a comma, a period, a colon, or parentheses instead.
- **Semicolons.** Rare in casual writing. Just use a period.
- **Colons.** Fine before a list. Avoid the "setup: reveal" habit.
- **Exclamation marks.** A couple are human. Zero in a casual piece can feel flat, and five in one paragraph feels like an ad.
- **Headers and bullets.** AI formats everything. For blog posts, emails, and reviews, write in paragraphs and use bullets only when the content really is a list.
- **Bold lead-ins on bullets** ("**Speed:** It's fast.") are a strong AI signature outside of reference docs like this one. Drop them in normal writing.
- **An emoji at the start of every bullet or heading.** Same problem.
- **Title Case On Every Heading.** Sentence case ("How we set it up") feels more natural.

## 9. Openings and endings

**Openings.** AI warms up: it sets context, explains why the topic matters, and previews what's coming. People usually start in the middle. Open with a specific moment, a number, a complaint, an opinion, or the answer itself.

- Instead of: "In this article, we'll explore five ways to improve your sleep."
- Try: "I used to fall asleep around 2 a.m. and wake up wrecked. Here's what actually changed that."

**Endings.** AI wraps up with a summary and an uplifting line ("By following these tips, you'll be well on your way to..."). People end with a next step, one last detail, a question they actually want answered, a small joke, or they just stop when they're done.

- Instead of: "In conclusion, choosing the right laptop depends on your individual needs and preferences."
- Try: "If you mostly write and browse, get the cheaper one. Seriously. Spend the difference on a decent chair."

## 10. Matching the register

"Informal" means different things in different places. Treat it like a dial.

| Context | Contractions | Fragments | Slang | First person | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Social posts, DMs | Always | Lots | OK (tbh, lol) | Yes | Short, reactive, lowercase is fine |
| Product reviews | Always | Some | Light | Yes | Details from actual use beat adjectives |
| Personal blog | Always | Some | Light | Yes | Stories, opinions, tangents |
| Business email | Usually | A few | None | Yes | Short and direct, skip the pleasantries |
| Marketing copy | Usually | Some | Very light | "We" and "you" | Concrete claims, no hype words |
| Reports, formal docs | Some | Rare | None | Sometimes | The human feel comes from specifics and clear opinions, not casual grammar |

## 11. Before and after examples

### Blog intro

**AI:**
> In today's fast-paced digital world, productivity apps have become more crucial than ever. Whether you're a seasoned professional or a budding entrepreneur, finding the right tool can be a game-changer. In this article, we'll delve into the top five apps that can help you unlock your full potential.

**Human:**
> I've tried maybe fifteen to-do apps in the last three years. Most lasted a week. Two stuck, and neither is the one everyone recommends. Here's what they do differently.

### Product review

**AI:**
> This coffee grinder offers exceptional performance and a sleek design. Its intuitive interface makes it easy to use, while its robust construction ensures long-lasting durability. Overall, it's a fantastic choice for coffee enthusiasts.

**Human:**
> I've used it every morning since March. The grind is way more even than my old blade grinder, no more half powder, half gravel. It's loud, though. My partner can hear it from the bedroom with the door shut. And the hopper only holds about 60 grams, so if you're making coffee for more than two people you'll be refilling it.

### Work email

**AI:**
> I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out to follow up on our previous discussion regarding the project timeline. Please don't hesitate to let me know if you have any questions or require further clarification.

**Human:**
> Hi Ken,
>
> Quick follow-up on Tuesday's call. Are we still good for the 14th on the first draft? If that's tight on your end, the 18th works for us too.
>
> Thanks,
> Sam

### Explaining something

**AI:**
> Composting is a sustainable practice that offers numerous environmental benefits. By diverting organic waste from landfills, it reduces methane emissions. Additionally, it produces nutrient-rich soil that enhances plant growth. Ultimately, composting is a simple yet powerful way to contribute to a healthier planet.

**Human:**
> Composting sounds like a lot of work. It mostly isn't. You throw food scraps in a bin, add dry leaves or torn-up cardboard so it doesn't turn into sludge, and stir it every week or two. In about three months you've got dark, crumbly soil your tomatoes will love. People worry about the smell, but if it smells bad you just need more cardboard.

## 12. Editing workflow

If you're starting from an AI draft, don't polish it. Rewrite it. Light editing keeps the skeleton, and the skeleton is exactly what detectors and readers pick up on.

1. **Gather your own material first.** Before writing, jot down the specifics: numbers, what happened, what you think, what annoyed you. Messy notes in any language are fine.
2. **Draft fast.** Write it the way you'd explain it to a coworker over lunch.
3. **Word pass.** Ctrl+F the words and phrases from section 3. Replace or delete them.
4. **Structure pass.** Remove "not just X, it's Y," triplets, -ing tails, and the summary ending. Cut headers and bullets you don't need.
5. **Rhythm pass.** Every paragraph should have at least one short sentence and one longer one. Change a couple of sentence openers.
6. **Specifics pass.** Every paragraph should contain one thing only you would know. If it doesn't, add one or cut the paragraph.
7. **Read it aloud.** Wherever you stumble, or it sounds like a brochure, rewrite that sentence the way you'd actually say it.

## 13. Final checklist

- [ ] No words from the AI list in section 3 (or only one or two, used naturally)
- [ ] No "In today's...", "Whether you're...", or "In conclusion"
- [ ] No "It's not X, it's Y"
- [ ] Not everything comes in threes
- [ ] Sentence lengths clearly vary within each paragraph
- [ ] Contractions throughout (for casual and semi-casual pieces)
- [ ] Two or three deliberate informal moves (a fragment, an And/But start, an aside)
- [ ] One em dash or none
- [ ] Concrete numbers, names, dates, or experiences
- [ ] At least one clear opinion
- [ ] The opening starts with something specific, not background
- [ ] The ending doesn't summarize
- [ ] Formatting is only as heavy as the content needs
- [ ] Read aloud once, and it sounds like a person talking

## 14. Honest notes on AI detectors

- **Scores are guesses, not proof.** Different tools often disagree a lot on the same text. A paragraph can score low on one and high on another.
- **Short texts are unreliable** either way, especially anything under about 200 words.
- **Non-native English writers get falsely flagged more often**, because careful second-language writing tends to be more predictable. A 2023 Stanford study (Liang et al.) found popular detectors labeled more than half of a set of essays by non-native writers as AI-generated. A high score on your own writing doesn't mean you did anything wrong.
- **Formulaic human writing gets flagged too**: legal text, templates, technical documentation.
- **Nothing guarantees 0%.** What helps most reliably is section 2: real specifics and real opinions. They're hard to fake statistically because they aren't the average of anything.
- **Skip the gimmicks.** Invisible characters, look-alike letters from other alphabets, and deliberate typos are increasingly caught, and they can break search, copy-paste, and readability.
- **Rules still apply.** If a school, publisher, client, or platform has rules about AI use or disclosure, a low score doesn't change them.
